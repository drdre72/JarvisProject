Technomancy: origins, practitioners and technological prophets
Technomancy has a considerably older intellectual ancestry than the word itself suggests. Although the modern term emerged around the beginning of the 1990s, its underlying philosophy can be traced back centuries through Hermeticism, mathematical mysticism, ceremonial magic, mechanical computation and the eventual development of cybernetics.

There are four distinct schools of thought worth investigating:

Divinatory technomancy — using technology to uncover hidden information or interpret patterns.

Operational technomancy — treating machines, programming languages and symbolic systems as instruments of human intention.

Artificial intelligence and technological mysticism — creating independent systems capable of reasoning, learning and adapting.

Technological prophecy — writers and inventors who anticipated technological capabilities that did not yet exist.

I'll distinguish documented scientific accomplishments from occult beliefs and philosophical speculation. There is substantial historical literature connecting these disciplines, but that does not establish supernatural efficacy.

The earliest published occurrence of technomancy I have been able to substantiate is Martindale's 1990 story. I cannot yet establish whether he personally coined the word or adopted it from an earlier, undocumented source.

Etymologically, it combines the Greek-derived concepts of technē, meaning art, craft or skill, and manteia, meaning divination or prophecy.

Its literal interpretation is closer to divination through technology than simply magic involving machines.

II. The ancestral technomancers
These individuals predate the term but developed systems that bear a remarkable conceptual resemblance to modern technomancy. They are not necessarily members of a continuous historical tradition.

1. Ramon Llull (1232–1316)
Raimondo Lullo (1233-1316). Beato tra le «ruote». A 700 anni dalla morte si ricorda il filosofo che con la sua «ars combinatoria» affascinò Bruno, Descartes e Leibniz, di Maria Bettetini - Diario
The mechanization of knowledge

Christian mystic, philosopher, logician and arguably one of the intellectual ancestors of general-purpose computation.

Llull developed rotating symbolic wheels that could generate arguments and propositions through a finite collection of fundamental principles. His ambition was to create a general method for discovering truths across disciplines.

His system, the Ars Magna, attempted to overcome linguistic barriers and make reasoning systematic.

Essential writings:

Ars Generalis Ultima (1305–1308)

Ars Brevis (1308)

Stanford's Encyclopedia of Philosophy recognizes the relationship between Llull's combinatorial reasoning and Leibniz's later conception of mechanical computation.

2. Ibn Khaldun (1332–1406) and the Zā'irja
İbn-i Haldun’a göre toplumun çöküş nedenlerine katılıyor musunuz? - KizlarSoruyor
The mechanical oracle

Ibn Khaldun documented an older Arabic divinatory system known as the Zā'irja.

It utilized structured matrices of letters, numbers, astrological symbols and combinatorial operations to generate answers to questions.

Interestingly, the answers could be expressed as Arabic poetry, making it an early example of a formalized system for generating linguistic outputs.

Some historians have proposed connections between this tradition and Llull's work, although the precise relationship remains uncertain.

Essential writing: The Muqaddimah (1377).

4. Athanasius Kircher (1602–1680)

The universal systems engineer

Jesuit polymath whose interests encompassed cryptography, mathematics, magnetism, linguistics, mechanical devices and theology.

He attempted to develop universal systems of knowledge and communication.

Kircher inherited aspects of Llull's combinatorial philosophy, exploring whether a universal symbolic language could reveal fundamental truths.

Essential writings: Polygraphia Nova (1663) and Ars Magna Sciendi (1669).

5. Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz (1646–1716)
Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz and calculation
The computational philosopher

Mathematician, philosopher and developer of binary arithmetic.

Leibniz envisioned a universal symbolic language and a corresponding calculus through which intellectual disagreements could be resolved using formal reasoning.

His proposed characteristica universalis and calculus ratiocinator are among the most significant philosophical antecedents of symbolic AI.

Essential writing: Dissertatio de Arte Combinatoria (1666).

III. Actual modern practitioners
These are individuals with documented involvement in occultism, technological development or the explicit synthesis of both.

6. Jack Parsons (1914–1952)
Jack Parsons: o gênio expulso da NASA por praticar ocultismo - Mega Curioso
Engineering and ceremonial magic

Parsons was a pioneering rocket propulsion researcher, an Aerojet co-founder and a participant in the experimental research group that became JPL.

Simultaneously, he was a practitioner of Aleister Crowley's Thelemic tradition.

Parsons is one of the strongest historical examples of an accomplished technological innovator who maintained a serious personal occult practice. His engineering achievements are documented independently of his magical beliefs.

8. Mark Pesce (1962–)
Mark Pesce
Digital technopaganism

Co-creator of the Virtual Reality Modeling Language (VRML) and a publicly documented technopagan practitioner.

Pesce explored cyberspace as a domain where imagination could acquire a programmable, navigable structure.

Of the contemporary practitioners, Pesce is arguably the closest match to the original spirit of modern technomancy.

His work extended beyond speculative writing into actual programming standards and virtual environments.

In 1995, he conducted CyberSamhain, a ceremony incorporating digital environments and pagan ritual.

Essential writing: The Playful World: How Technology Is Transforming Our Imagination (2000).

What makes this interesting is that it does not require abandoning scientific methodology. These older conceptual frameworks can be treated as sources of architectural hypotheses, subsequently implemented and tested through engineering.

Three individuals I would investigate particularly deeply:

Mark Pesce, for the actual practice of technological mysticism.

Stanisław Lem, for the intellectual foresight of what artificial intelligence could become.

Ramon Llull, for the attempt to construct a universal reasoning mechanism centuries before electronic computation.

Of everything uncovered here, I find Llull's work particularly relevant to Horizon. The attempt to derive general reasoning capabilities from a finite collection of symbolic primitives, without depending on an enormous corpus of linguistic material, is a research direction worth examining in its own right.

The central question behind technomancy, stripped of its supernatural assumptions, might be expressed as follows:

To what extent can intention be encoded into a symbolic system that subsequently produces intelligent, autonomous and measurable changes in reality?

That is a legitimate engineering question—and one with a surprisingly long history.
